Hoopr, Find your next pickup run

The Problem

Finding pickup basketball is harder than it should be

If you want to play pickup basketball, your options are basically: show up to a court and hope people are there, or rely on a group chat. There's no reliable way to know if a court has a game running, what skill level is playing, or how to find people to run with in a new city.

I ran into this myself after moving to Seattle. That gave me two kinds of users to design for: players looking for a game, and the people willing to host one. The app only works if hosts post games and players show up to them, so both sides had to be easy from day one.

The problem isn't that courts don't exist. It's that you never know what's happening there until you show up.

Scoping the first version

One loop, built end to end, before anything else

As the PM, my first job was deciding what not to build. I defined the smallest version that would actually get a game played and recorded, and made that loop work completely before adding anything around it.

01

Find a court

02

Host or join a rally

03

Play the game

04

Log the score

In the first version: a map of nearby courts, court details, hosting a rally with a time, format, skill level, and open spots, joining a rally, logging the final score, and a player profile with game history.

Held for later: notifications when a rally is posted near you, deeper social features, and anything that didn't directly help a game get played. Each of these is useful, but none of them matter if the core loop doesn't work.

This kept the build small enough to ship as a side project, and it meant every real game played on Hoopr tested the whole product, not just one screen.

Design Decisions

Why it looks and works the way it does

Map first, not list first. Early exploration considered a list of courts, but where a court is relative to you is core to the decision. The map is always the entry point.

Orange on a dark map. Orange reads as basketball without explanation, and dark map tiles keep the courts the brightest thing on screen.

Skill level up front. The biggest worry for pickup players isn't finding a court, it's showing up to the wrong level of game. "Near my level" is a top-level filter, not buried in settings, and every rally shows its level before you join.

Score after the game, not during it. Live scoring would mean someone on their phone while playing. Instead, the final score is entered once the game ends, and players are assigned to Team A or Team B with a single tap each, so logging a game takes seconds.

Color

Color palette

Every color in Hoopr has a single defined use, so nothing competes with the action on screen. Orange is reserved for what you tap. Green and red only ever mean a win or a loss, never anything else.

How It Fits Together

Four objects that everything is built on

Thinking about Hoopr as a technical PM meant defining the handful of things the app keeps track of, and how they connect. Every screen is a view onto one or more of these.

ObjectWhat it holdsWhere you see it
CourtLocation, court type (full or half), number of courts, rating, crowd reportsMap, court detail
RallyCourt, host, time, format (like 1v1), skill level, open spots, statusCourt detail, profile history
PlayerHandle, skill tier, followers, games, wins, best streakProfile, team assignment
ResultFinal score and which players were on each teamRecent rallies, win and loss history

Keeping the model small keeps every feature connected. The same results that appear in a court's recent rallies also make up a player's history, wins, and best streak. The "near my level" filter matches a rally's skill level to the player's tier. One game, logged once, shows up everywhere it matters.

A rally also moves through a clear set of stages, and each stage decides what the app shows:

Designing for Real Data

Edge cases aren't edge cases when the games are real

Hoopr is different from my other case studies because it's a product I actually use. The rallies, scores, and players in these screens are real, not placeholder content, and real use surfaced states a prototype never would:

  • Games that never got a score. Some rallies ended without anyone entering a result, and some were saved as 0–0. Instead of hiding them or counting them as losses, they show as "Done" in a player's history, so the record stays honest without breaking anyone's stats.
  • Players who join and then leave. A rally has to handle people dropping out, so the open spots a player sees stay accurate.
  • Brand new courts and players. A court with no crowd reports and a profile with zero followers are the normal starting state, so both have clear empty states and a next step, like "Report" or "Follow."

Working through these made the product much more reliable, and it changed how I scope work: I now plan for the unfinished and messy versions of a flow from the start, not after launch.

Measuring Success

How I judge whether the core loop works

Because the whole product is one loop, the right measures are about whether games actually happen and get recorded, not about downloads.

  • Rallies that fill: the share of posted rallies that reach their open spots. This tells me whether hosting is worth a host's time.
  • Games that get a score: the share of ended rallies with a logged result, versus ones marked Done. This tests whether post-game scoring is fast enough.
  • Players who come back: how many players join a second rally. This is the clearest sign the app is useful beyond a first try.

What's Next

The core loop works. The growth loop needs more.

Hoopr does its main job well: you can find a game, play it, and see it on your record. What it doesn't do yet is bring people back on its own. The next priorities follow directly from that:

  • Notifications for nearby rallies, so players hear about a game without having to open the app and check.
  • Deeper social features around the players you follow, so a good run turns into a regular one.

The biggest lesson from building Hoopr as both designer and PM: the product decisions and the design decisions are the same decisions. Choosing to score after the game was a design choice, a scope choice, and a data choice all at once.
